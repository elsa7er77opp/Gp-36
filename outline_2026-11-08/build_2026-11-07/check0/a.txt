1. INTRODUCTION

   1.1 Project Background and Justification
Electric micro-mobility vehicles such as e-scooters, small delivery robots and personal mobility
pods are now common in many cities, and they share the road with pedestrians, cars and a
road surface that is not always in good condition. A small vehicle is much more affected by
potholes and speed bumps than a car, and its rider or its control system has very little time to
react. Most low-cost safety systems use a single sensor. An ultrasonic sensor alone cannot tell
a soft bush from a solid wall, and a camera alone can fail in poor lighting or when the lens is
blocked, so the brief of this project asks for a module that combines several sensors.

A review of perception for intelligent two-wheelers by Hassanin et al. (2025) found fewer than
ten e-scooter detection studies in three years, small datasets, and weak performance at night,
and it named multi-modal fusion as a future direction. Existing systems that do work are
expensive: Zheng et al. (2025) used an Intel RealSense depth camera on an e-scooter, and
Barua and Hitosugi (2025) used thermal and stereo cameras on an NVIDIA Jetson AGX Orin.
On the other hand, the low-cost ESP32-CAM and ultrasonic system of Aniobi (2024) had a
delay of about 715 ms, which is too slow for a moving vehicle. There is therefore a gap for a low-
cost, modular perception pod that can detect obstacles and road hazards, measure their
distance and give a clear safety decision in real time.

The expected benefits are a cheap and re-usable "perception pod" that can be mounted on
different small vehicles, a documented and leak-free dataset and model for potholes and speed
bumps, and measured results (accuracy, latency and distance error) that can be compared with
the literature. The work also supports the safety of micro-mobility users in local conditions,
where unmarked speed bumps and potholes are very common.


   1.2 Project Aim and Objectives
Aim: To design, build and evaluate a low-cost multi-modal perception pod (camera, LiDAR and
ultrasonic range sensors, and IMU) for a small micro-mobility vehicle that detects obstacles and
road hazards (pedestrians, vehicles, potholes and speed bumps), estimates their distance and
outputs SAFE / WARNING / STOP decisions in real time.

Objectives (each one is linked to the PDS in Table 1 and to the Gantt chart in Appendix A):

   1.4 Methodology (VDI 2206)
The project follows the VDI 2206 V-model for mechatronic systems (VDI, 2021), which was also
used in the Integrated Engineering Systems Design module. The left side of the V goes from

                                                                                                                        2
School of Engineering and Computer Science                                                BEng Project Outline

requirements to system design and then to domain-specific design (mechanical, electrical and
software/AI). The right side integrates the domains and checks the result against the design
and the PDS (Figure 1). Modelling and analysis, such as a latency versus stopping-distance
model in Simulink and an FEA of the mount, run beside these stages.




                    Figure 1: VDI 2206 V-model as applied to the perception pod

For the AI part, a strict evaluation method is used: the data is split by video or recording group,
so frames from one drive never appear in both training and test; near-duplicate images are
removed with image hashing (also under flips and rotations); the model and thresholds are
chosen on the validation set only; and the test set is used once per final model. Precision,
recall, mAP50 and accuracy are always reported per class. This method was adopted after an
audit showed that the first test mAP50 of 0.69 was inflated (42 test images were identical to
training images and 100 were near-duplicates), so those numbers were withdrawn. The
corrected results are in Section 4.1 (Table 7).

   1.6 System Architecture and Circuit
Figure 2 shows the selected architecture. The ESP32-CAM streams video to the laptop, where
two YOLO11n models run on the same frame: a hazard model trained in this project and a
pretrained COCO model for people and vehicles. The ESP32 DevKit is the sensor hub on the
vehicle: it reads the TF-Luna (UART), the two HC-SR04 sensors and the two MPU6050 IMUs
and OLED (I2C), sends the readings to the laptop, and can stop the motors through the L298N
driver when a STOP flag arrives or the link is lost. Fusion is done at decision level, which is the
lightest method for low-cost hardware (Yeong et al., 2021): the detection box gives the class
and direction, the range sensor gives the distance, and the IMU confirms bumps and rough
surfaces. The full pin-level wiring with the power supply is given in Appendix D.

   1.7 Health, Safety and Ethics
Health and safety. The main hazards and controls are: (1) Li-ion 18650 cells – a 2S BMS
protects against over-charge, over-discharge and short circuit; charging only with the 8.4 V
charger and under supervision. (2) Moving vehicle – speed limited to about 1 m/s, a main power
switch and a software STOP, tests only in the lab or a closed area of the campus, never on
public roads. (3) Soldering and 3D printing – done in the lab with ventilation, heat-resistant mat
and lead-free solder. (4) TF-Luna LiDAR – a Class 1 eye-safe laser product (IEC 60825-1), the
sensor is still not looked into at close range. (5) Electrical – all voltages are below 9 V. A risk
assessment of these hazards is given in Appendix C.

Ethics. The project does not involve measurements on human subjects or questionnaires

School of Engineering and Computer Science                                      BEng Project Outline

(Appendix C, Q8–Q10 are "No"). People and vehicles are detected with a pretrained COCO
model, so images of people are not collected for training. Road hazard images come from
public datasets with open licences (for example CC BY 4.0), and every source is credited. If
the pod camera records people on campus during tests, faces and number plates will be blurred
before storage, the data will be kept only on a password-protected laptop and its backup, and
no identifiable images will be published. If recording of people becomes necessary, Ethics
Committee approval will be requested first.

Legal requirements. Images in which people can be identified are personal data under the UK
Data Protection Act 2018 (UK GDPR) and the Egyptian Personal Data Protection Law No. 151
of 2020 (Egypt, 2020), so they are blurred, kept private and deleted at the end of the project.
Dataset licences are followed (e.g. CC BY 4.0 attribution), the LiDAR is classified under IEC
60825-1, and e-waste goes through the university route.


   1.8 Sustainability Analysis
 • Re-use of hardware: the vehicle is borrowed from the lab and the pod is modular, so it can
    be moved to another vehicle after the project; no new chassis is bought.

 • Low-power electronics: the ESP32 boards use much less energy than a single-board
    computer, and the existing laptop is used for AI instead of buying new hardware.

 • Materials: the sensor tower is printed in PLA with low infill and few supports; failed prints
    and off-cuts are recycled through the lab where possible.

 • Batteries and e-waste: rechargeable cells with a BMS extend battery life; damaged cells
    and electronics will be disposed of through the university e-waste route.

 • Computing energy: public datasets are re-used instead of long data-collection drives, and
    training runs are planned in advance and compared on the validation set, so the GPU is
    not used for random experiments.

 • Social: the result can improve the safety of micro-mobility users and pedestrians at low
    cost.
